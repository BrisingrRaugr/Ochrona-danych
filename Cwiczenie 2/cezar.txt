The Night Ferry

The night ferry left the harbour at eleven, as it had done every evening for as long as anyone in the town could remember. Marta stood at the rail on the upper deck and watched the lights of the quay shrink into a thin yellow line. Below her, the engines settled into their slow, patient rhythm, and the smell of diesel mixed with salt and wet rope.

She had taken this crossing twice a year since she was a child: once in early spring, when the sea was grey and restless, and once at the end of summer, when it was so calm that the ferry seemed to slide across a sheet of dark glass. Tonight it was neither. A light wind came from the west, and small waves slapped against the hull with a sound like distant applause.

In the lounge, a group of students had taken over two tables. They were playing cards and arguing about the rules, which seemed to change every few minutes. An old man in a green coat sat alone by the window, writing in a notebook with a short pencil. Every so often he stopped, looked out at the black water, and then went back to writing, as if the sea were dictating something to him one sentence at a time.

Marta bought a cup of tea from the small counter at the back. The woman serving it had a badge with her name, Ilse, and the tired smile of someone who had answered the same questions thousands of times. "Calm night," Marta said. "For now," Ilse answered, and pointed at a screen above the counter. It showed a simple map: the coastline, a dotted line for the route, and a tiny arrow for the ferry, already a quarter of the way across.

Back on deck, the wind had grown a little stronger. The lighthouse on the northern cape blinked twice, paused, and blinked twice again. Marta counted the seconds between the flashes without thinking about it. Her grandfather had taught her that every lighthouse along this coast had its own pattern, so that a sailor who knew the patterns could tell exactly where he was, even on a night without stars. It was, he used to say, a kind of secret language that everyone was allowed to learn.

Around one o'clock the island appeared: first a faint glow, then a cluster of lights, and finally the dark outline of the hills behind the port. The engines changed their tone. Crew members in orange jackets walked along the deck, checking ropes and calling to each other in short, practiced phrases. The students packed their cards away. The old man closed his notebook, put the pencil behind his ear, and stood up slowly, holding the back of his chair.

When the ramp finally came down, Marta was one of the last to leave. She paused at the top, looked back at the empty deck, and listened for a moment to the quiet ticking of the cooling engines. Then she picked up her bag and walked down into the island night, where the air smelled of pine trees and the only sound was the sea.
